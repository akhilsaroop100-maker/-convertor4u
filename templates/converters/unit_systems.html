01

Why unit systems exist

A unit is an agreed standard used to describe a quantity. A measurement combines a number with that standard: 3 meters, 12 inches, or 20 degrees Celsius. Shared units let measurements be repeated, compared, and communicated.

Measurement systems developed independently across regions and trades. Modern conversion connects these conventions without changing the physical quantity being described.

02

International System of Units

The International System of Units, abbreviated SI, is the modern metric system and the principal international standard. Its seven base units include the meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela.

SI scales cleanly through powers of ten. Prefixes such as milli-, centi-, and kilo- make related sizes straightforward: 1 kilometer equals 1,000 meters, while 1 centimeter equals 0.01 meter.

03

US customary units

The United States commonly uses customary units alongside SI. Familiar examples include inches, feet, yards, miles, ounces, pounds, US fluid ounces, cups, pints, quarts, and gallons.

Some names overlap with Imperial units but represent different quantities—especially volume—so the system and regional definition matter.

04

Imperial units

The Imperial system was formally established in Britain in the nineteenth century. Miles, yards, feet, inches, stones, pounds, pints, and gallons remain familiar in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, although SI is used officially for most purposes.

An Imperial gallon is not the same as a US gallon. Convertor4U labels regional units explicitly wherever ambiguity could affect the result.

CONVERSION PRINCIPLE

Fixed units are converted through a defined base unit. Temperature uses scale and offset formulas. Currency uses current, dated reference rates and should not be treated as a transaction quote.
